This document contains detailed opportunity profiles for three early-stage ventures identified and actively supported by True Potential across Tamil-majority regions of Sri Lanka. ### The Problem

Northern Sri Lanka is living through a mental health emergency that has persisted, largely invisible, for over 15 years since the end of the civil war. The scale is staggering and well-documented in peer-reviewed research:

- **58.8% of adults** attending primary care in Northern Province screen positive for at least one mental health disorder - PTSD (13.7%), depression (41.1%), or anxiety (46.7%). This comes from a cross-sectional study of internally displaced adults published in BMC Psychiatry.
- A broader collective trauma study across five war-affected districts found even higher prevalence: **PTSD at 46.8%** and **depression at 57.1%**.
- **One in four children** in the Northeast meets diagnostic criteria for PTSD.
- **80% of those suffering receive no treatment at all** - not partial treatment, not inadequate treatment, but literally nothing.

The supply side is equally broken. Sri Lanka has approximately **one psychiatrist per 500,000 people** nationally, but the distribution is catastrophically skewed: **60% of all psychiatrists** are concentrated in the Western Province (Colombo). Kilinochchi, Mullaitivu, and Mannar have had **zero consultant psychiatrists** at various points in recent years. The surgical unit at Mullaitivu District Hospital **closed entirely** for lack of a surgeon. The 2022 economic crisis triggered a mass exodus of medical professionals - **1,700+ medical officers left Sri Lanka** between 2022 and 2024 - and Northern Province, already the most underserved, bore the heaviest losses.

The 2022 crisis also triggered a **second wave of psychological distress**. Suicide rates spiked 23% in Northern Province as the economic collapse layered financial trauma onto unresolved conflict trauma. The combination of intergenerational PTSD, economic despair, and zero access to professional care creates a compounding crisis that worsens with each passing year.

**There is no Tamil-language digital mental health platform anywhere in the world.** Sri Lanka has 129 HealthTech startups (per Tracxn), but all are Colombo-centric and operate in English or Sinhala. oDoc (teleconsultation, 200K+ users) and Flash Health (cashless outpatient care, $200K seed round January 2026) are the closest analogues, but neither serves Tamil-speaking populations, addresses mental health specifically, or has any presence in Northern or Eastern Province. The 5 million Tamil speakers in Sri Lanka and 1 million+ in the global diaspora have zero culturally appropriate digital mental health support.

---

### The Thesis

Maruththuvam (from the Tamil word for "medicine/healing") is building a mobile-first, Tamil-native mental health platform that delivers structured psychosocial support, therapist-matching, and AI-assisted screening directly to the smartphones that 92% of Vanni-region residents already own.

The core insight is that while Northern Sri Lanka has essentially zero specialist mental health infrastructure, it now has near-universal smartphone penetration (up from ~40% in 2018) and 85–90% 4G coverage via Dialog and Mobitel. The delivery mechanism exists - what's missing is the content, the clinical framework, and the trusted brand.

**The care model is hybrid by design:**

1. **AI-assisted screening layer** - Validated Tamil-language adaptations of PHQ-9 (depression), GAD-7 (anxiety), and PCL-5 (PTSD) instruments delivered via the app. This provides immediate triage and self-awareness for users who may not recognise their symptoms as treatable conditions - critical in a culture where mental health stigma remains severe.

2. **Structured self-help programmes** - Evidence-based CBT and trauma-processing modules delivered as guided audio/visual content in Tamil. Designed for low-literacy accessibility with voice narration and visual storytelling.

3. **Community health worker network** - Maruththuvam trains existing MOH (Medical Officer of Health) community health workers as digital navigators. These workers - already trusted figures in rural villages - help users onboard, interpret screening results, and escalate to clinical care when needed. This is the critical trust bridge that pure digital products cannot replicate in post-conflict settings.

4. **Therapist-matching and teleconsultation** - For users requiring professional support, the platform matches them with registered counsellors and clinical psychologists via video call. Initial supply comes from a partnership with Jaffna Teaching Hospital's psychiatry unit and a network of diaspora Tamil mental health professionals willing to offer pro-bono hours.

**Revenue model - three tiers:**

| Tier | Price | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Community (free) | £0 - funded by INGO grants (GIZ, USAID) | Conflict-affected populations, walk-in users |
| Individual Premium | ~£1.40/month (LKR 500) | Working adults seeking structured support |
| B2B Employer Wellness | £3–5/employee/month | Garment factories (MAS, Brandix), BOI zone companies, Jaffna-based businesses |

The B2B angle is underappreciated. The Northern Province has seen significant garment manufacturing investment (MAS Holdings operates multiple factories). These employers face high absenteeism and turnover driven by untreated mental health conditions - a wellness product that reduces attrition has clear ROI.

### The Problem

Northern Sri Lanka's agricultural economy is trapped in a devastating cycle: farmers grow food, a third of it rots, and they keep less than a quarter of what consumers eventually pay. The numbers are damning at every level:

**Post-harvest losses destroy enormous value.** Sri Lanka loses an estimated **£160 million annually** in post-harvest fruit and vegetable waste - with loss rates reaching **20–40% for vegetables and 30–40% for fruits**. The causes are structural: no integrated cold-chain infrastructure, open-truck transport in tropical heat (average temperatures of 30–35°C), and a fragmented network of middlemen who lack any incentive to invest in preservation. In the Northern Province, the problem is compounded by decades of infrastructure destruction during the war - cold storage facilities, packing houses, and market infrastructure were either destroyed or never built in the first place.

**Farmers are price-takers squeezed by middlemen.** The typical supply chain from a Northern Province farm to a Colombo consumer involves 2–4 intermediaries, each taking margin. Only **1% of Sri Lanka's fresh produce flows through organised supermarket channels** - the rest moves through informal wholesale markets (polas) where price discovery is opaque and farmers have zero bargaining power. A study of Nuwara Eliya vegetable farmers found that a farmer must sell four kilograms of carrots to afford one kilogram of rice, while Colombo consumers pay 3–4x the farm-gate price for those same carrots.

**Jaffna's agricultural base is under existential pressure.** Groundwater salinity has caused the **permanent abandonment of 43% of Jaffna's paddy land** - approximately 8,178 hectares. Over-extraction from the peninsula's 19,198 agro-wells has exceeded aquifer recharge rates, and salinity has increased 1.6-fold over two decades. The 2021 fertiliser ban (reversed in 2022) compounded the damage: rice production fell 20% nationally, tea yields dropped 26.8%, and 54% of farmers lost over half their harvest. Farmers in Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu face the additional challenge of farming on recently demined land - productive, but requiring investment in soil rehabilitation and new crop planning.

**The palmyrah economy is artisanal and fragmented.** Jaffna's **115 million palmyrah trees** - 95% of Sri Lanka's total - support over 12,000 workers and produce approximately 800 products (toddy, jaggery, odiyal flour, fibre, timber, vinegar). But the industry is almost entirely informal, with no cold chain, no quality standardisation, no export certification, and no brand presence. The Palmyrah Development Board exists but operates on minimal budget. The palmyrah represents one of Northern Sri Lanka's most distinctive and under-commercialised agricultural assets.

---

### The Thesis

Vayal (Tamil for "field" or "farmland") is building an asset-light cold-chain network that connects Northern Province farmer cooperatives directly to Colombo buyers - cutting out middlemen, reducing post-harvest losses from 35% to under 10%, and increasing farmer price realisation by 40–60%.

**The physical infrastructure:**

- **Solar-powered micro cold rooms** at farm-gate collection points - 2-tonne capacity units that cost approximately £3,000–4,000 each to deploy. These are not full cold storage warehouses; they're village-level holding facilities that keep produce at optimal temperature for 24–48 hours until collection. Solar power is critical because grid electricity in rural Kilinochchi is unreliable (4–6 hour daily outages are common).

- **GPS-tracked refrigerated transport** on the rebuilt A9 highway - the arterial road connecting Jaffna to Colombo, which was a frontline during the war and only fully restored in the mid-2010s. The journey is now approximately 6 hours (down from 12+ before restoration), making same-day perishable logistics viable for the first time since 1990.

- **Hub-and-spoke model** - village-level micro cold rooms feed into a district-level aggregation hub (initially in Kilinochchi town), where produce is sorted, graded, and loaded onto refrigerated trucks for Colombo. This structure allows Vayal to aggregate small-farmer volumes into commercially viable loads.

**The digital layer:**

- **B2B marketplace** connecting farmer cooperatives with Colombo supermarkets (Keells, Cargills, Arpico), hotel chains (Jetwing, Cinnamon, Shangri-La), restaurant groups, and export agents
- Real-time price discovery - farmers see what Colombo buyers are paying, eliminating information asymmetry
- Order matching - buyers can place forward orders based on projected harvest volumes, giving farmers demand visibility and reducing speculative planting

**The palmyrah play:**

Vayal is positioning palmyrah value-added products - particularly jaggery, odiyal flour, and palmyrah vinegar - as premium, export-grade SKUs. The goal is to achieve organic certification and build a branded product line targeting the Tamil diaspora grocery market (an estimated 200+ Tamil-owned grocery shops in London alone, plus significant retail presence in Toronto, Paris, and Sydney). This transforms palmyrah from an artisanal subsistence product into a branded export commodity.

2. **Demining has unlocked prime agricultural land.** HALO Trust has cleared over **300,000 anti-personnel mines** and returned **120+ km²** of land to Northern communities since 2010. Contamination fell from 506 km² (2010) to 25.8 km² (2018), and the northern sector of Muhamalai - one of the war's most heavily mined frontlines - was declared fully cleared in 2025. Kilinochchi and Mullaitivu now have large tracts of fertile, recently cleared farmland available for cultivation - but farmers need market access and cold-chain support to make this land commercially productive.

3. **Organised retail is expanding but supply chains haven't kept up.** Sri Lanka's supermarket penetration is rising rapidly post-crisis as Keells, Cargills, and Arpico expand beyond Colombo. These retailers want reliable, graded, consistently supplied produce - but currently only 1% of fresh produce flows through organised channels. The aggregation and quality assurance opportunity is enormous. Vayal is positioned to be the procurement partner that enables Northern Province integration into this expanding retail network.

### The Problem

The civil war didn't just destroy buildings and displace people - it destroyed an education system. Three decades of conflict, followed by a botched reconstruction and then economic collapse, have left Tamil-medium students in the Northern and Eastern Provinces with the worst educational outcomes in the country. The data is unambiguous:

**Teacher shortages are catastrophic.** The Eastern Province alone lacks **~3,698 teachers** - including 222 mathematics teachers, 128 science teachers, and 69 English teachers at secondary level. The Northern Province has a gap of approximately 2,900 teachers. These aren't rounding errors; these are fundamental gaps in the ability to deliver a curriculum. Teachers posted to rural Northern and Eastern schools - often involuntarily - frequently attend only **3–3.5 days per week** while commuting from or seeking transfers to Colombo. The result is that students in places like Mullaitivu, Kilinochchi, and rural Batticaloa often have no qualified STEM teacher for weeks at a time.

**Exam outcomes reflect the crisis.** Mullaitivu is the **poorest-performing district** of Sri Lanka's 25 districts on national O-Level and A-Level examinations. Nationally, only **40.2% of 17–18 year olds remain in school** - but in conflict-affected Tamil districts, dropout rates are significantly higher. The pipeline from secondary school to university is severely constrained: the historical standardisation policy (1971–present in various forms) required Tamil students to score higher than Sinhala students for university admission in science streams, and while the most egregious aspects have been reformed, its legacy persists. Northern Province's share of science-based university entrants fell from **27.5% to 7%** between 1969 and 1974 - a structural inequity that has never been fully reversed.

**The brain drain is self-reinforcing.** Over **50% of state university graduates** emigrate permanently, rising to **80–90% in medicine, engineering, and agriculture**. This means the few Tamil students who beat the odds and gain STEM qualifications then leave - draining the very talent pool that could staff the schools and hospitals. The remaining teachers are disproportionately older, less digitally literate, and approaching retirement.

**EdTech ignores Tamil speakers.** Sri Lanka's private EdTech platforms - eLearning.lk, eSiphala.lk, and various tuition platforms - are **overwhelmingly Sinhala-medium**. The government's e-Thaksalawa platform provides free curriculum-aligned content in Tamil, Sinhala, and English for Grades 1–13, reaching 700,000+ daily users - but Tamil content development lags significantly behind Sinhala for advanced STEM subjects (A-Level physics, chemistry, combined maths). A British Council report found that students leaving school lack expected English proficiency, and **70% of the rural population** cannot effectively access internet-based services in English. The result: Tamil-medium STEM students have no quality digital supplementary content in their language.

**The digital access paradox.** A 2024 study of the Vanni region found **92.3% smartphone penetration** but only **12% computer ownership**. Mobile internet reaches 85–90% of households via 4G, but fixed broadband is essentially absent from villages. Eastern Province has the **lowest computer ownership in Sri Lanka** at just 11.9% of households. Email proficiency stands at ~50%, and only 17% can navigate employment websites. This means any EdTech solution must be mobile-first and optimised for smartphone viewing - desktop or laptop-based platforms simply cannot reach this population.

---

### The Thesis

Kalvi (the Tamil word for "education") delivers curriculum-aligned, Tamil-medium STEM content via short-form video lessons (5–10 minutes each) optimised for smartphone viewing - the only device the vast majority of target students actually own.

**The content model:**

- **Short-form video lessons** covering the full O-Level and A-Level STEM syllabus (mathematics, physics, chemistry, biology) in Tamil medium - aligned precisely to the Sri Lankan national curriculum and examination standards
- Produced by **top Tamil-medium teachers** recruited from Jaffna and Batticaloa - teachers who consistently produce students scoring A's and B's at national level, and who can be paid content creation fees well above their government salary
- **Low-data-optimised** - videos compressed for 3G/4G streaming, available for offline download, and designed for small-screen viewing. Each lesson is self-contained and re-watchable
- Supplemented by **AI-generated practice problems** and adaptive quizzing - the system identifies knowledge gaps and serves targeted practice sets calibrated to Sri Lankan exam standards

**Why video, why short-form?**

The answer comes from observing how Tamil students actually learn today. The dominant supplementary education model in Sri Lanka is private tuition - teachers running after-school classes for 20–100 students, charging LKR 500–2,000/month. This is effectively a video lecture model already, just delivered in person. Kalvi digitises and democratises it - making the best teachers accessible to every student, regardless of whether their school has a qualified teacher or not.

Short-form (5–10 minutes) is critical because mobile data is metered and expensive relative to incomes. A student watching a 60-minute lecture at 480p consumes ~500MB; the same content split into 5-minute focused segments at optimised quality uses a fraction of the data and can be consumed in gaps during the school day, commute, or evening.

**Revenue model - three streams:**

| Stream | Price | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Freemium Consumer | Free basic / LKR 300/month premium (~£0.73) | Sri Lankan Tamil-medium students |
| B2B School Licence | £200–500/school/year | Provincial education departments, private schools, NGO-funded school programmes |

The diaspora revenue model is the critical insight that makes Kalvi's economics work. UK and Canadian Tamil parents - many of whom want their children to maintain Tamil-medium competency alongside English education - will pay developed-market prices for quality Tamil-medium STEM content. Even 1,000 diaspora subscribers at £9.99/month generates ~£10,000/month in hard currency - enough to fund free access for 10,000+ students in Sri Lanka. This cross-subsidy model is Kalvi's structural advantage.

### Why Now

1. **Policy window for EdTech procurement.** Sri Lanka's 2025 national education policy explicitly prioritises digital learning and provincial equity - creating formal procurement pathways for EdTech solutions in underserved districts for the first time. Provincial education departments now have budget lines and mandate to purchase digital supplementary content. This didn't exist before 2025.

2. **Mobile infrastructure has caught up with device penetration.** Mobile data costs in Sri Lanka dropped 40%+ since 2022, and Dialog's 4G coverage now reaches 85–90% of Northern and Eastern households. Combined with 92% smartphone penetration, the infrastructure for mobile-first content delivery is in place. The constraint is content, not connectivity.

3. **Brain drain makes digital supplementation essential, not optional.** The post-crisis emigration wave has accelerated teacher shortages to breaking point - 80–90% of STEM graduates leave permanently. The 3,700 teacher vacancies in the Eastern Province are not going to be filled by conventional recruitment. Digital content isn't a nice-to-have; it is becoming the primary mechanism by which Tamil-medium students access quality STEM instruction.

# Appendix: Regional & Macro Context

For investors unfamiliar with the Sri Lankan context, the following data points underpin the thesis across all three companies.

### Post-war recovery (since 2009)

- 26-year civil war ended May 2009; Northern and Eastern Provinces bore the heaviest destruction
- HALO Trust has cleared 300,000+ anti-personnel mines, returning 120+ km² to communities
- 280,000 displaced people have returned to the Northern Province
- Infrastructure rebuilt: A9 highway, Northern rail line, Jaffna International Airport (reopened 2019)
- Jaffna's tech ecosystem - anchored by Yarl IT Hub - has catalysed 60+ companies since 2010

### 2022 economic crisis and recovery

- Sri Lanka defaulted on sovereign debt in April 2022 - the country's first-ever default
- GDP contracted ~9.5% cumulatively in 2022–2023; inflation peaked at 69.8% (September 2022)
- IMF Extended Fund Facility ($3 billion) approved March 2023; four reviews completed, ~$1.74 billion disbursed
- GDP grew +5.0% in 2024; inflation collapsed to near-zero; rupee appreciated 19.4% from trough
- Foreign reserves rebuilt from effectively zero to $6.5 billion (3 months import cover)
- Debt restructuring 94% complete - $3 billion forgiven, $25 billion restructured

### Northern Province investment environment

- BOI minimum investment threshold: $50,000 in Northern Province (vs. $250,000 for Colombo)
- 200% capital allowances on top of normal depreciation for Northern investments
- Achchuveli Industrial Estate (65 acres, near Jaffna): India-funded green industrial zone, 50+ companies interested
- Northern Investment Summit 2026 (January 2026, Jaffna): 400+ delegates, plans for permanent Northern Investment Forum
- Key constraint: only $25 million of Sri Lanka's ~$15 billion exports originates from the North

### Tamil diaspora

- UK Sri Lankan Tamil population: 100,000–200,000 (concentrated in London - Harrow, East Ham, Tooting, Redbridge)
- Global Sri Lankan Tamil diaspora: 700,000–1.2 million (Canada, UK, India, Germany, Switzerland, Australia)
- Heavily professional: strong representation in medicine, law, accountancy, banking
- Remittances to Sri Lanka: $6.58 billion in 2024 (6.4% of GDP) - but almost entirely consumption remittances, not investment
- No dedicated Sri Lankan Tamil diaspora venture fund exists - this is the binding constraint
- Key organisations: British Tamil Chamber of Commerce, Tamil Diaspora Alliance, InvestJaffna.com
